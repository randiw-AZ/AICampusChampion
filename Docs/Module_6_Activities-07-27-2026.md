### Reading 1: [Cultivating Communities of Practice](https://facilitateadultlearning.pbworks.com/f/7Principles_CoP.pdf) — Chapter 1 Excerpt (Wenger, McDermott & Snyder, 2002\)

What to look for as you read:

* The three structural elements of a community of practice: DOMAIN (shared area of knowledge/concern), COMMUNITY (relationships enabling learning), and PRACTICE (shared repertoire of approaches, tools, stories). All three must be present for a CoP to function.  
* The distinction between a community of practice and other organizational groupings: a team (defined by task), a network (defined by relationship), a community of interest (defined by shared topic without shared practice). What makes AI Campus Champions a potential CoP rather than just a network?  
* The 'value creation' argument: Wenger et al. argue that CoPs create value at three levels — individual learning, team performance, and organizational capability. Which level of value does your initiative primarily target?  
* The concept of 'cultivating' rather than 'building': CoPs cannot be created by mandate — they must be cultivated by creating the conditions for organic participation. What does this imply for how you design and launch your initiative?

Guided reading questions — annotate answers as you go:

* Q1: What are the three structural elements of a community of practice? Using these three elements, describe whether the AI Campus Champions workshop cohort currently meets the definition of a CoP — or falls short on one or more elements.

Answer: The three structural elements of a community of practice are **domain** (shared area of knowledge/concern), **community** (relationships enabling learning), and **practice** (shared repertoire of approaches, tools, stories). I think the AI Campus Champions provides all 3, although not all have the same level of success. The domain is AI, the community is our cohort, and the practice is the products we are able to share with our academic unit. 

* Q2: Wenger et al. distinguish between 'core members' (active contributors), 'active members' (regular participants), and 'peripheral members' (observers). How should an AI Campus Champion initiative accommodate all three participation levels without requiring everyone to become a core member?

Answer: The AI Campus Champion initiative should not expect equal participation from all members. Instead, it should allow for varying degrees of engagement. Not all learning happens in the public spaces. And not all members of the community feel comfortable sharing in the public spaces. The initiative should provide and value alternate spaces. Learning can occur during individual tasks, one-on-one conversations, within the group, and outside the group. 

* Q3: The book argues that CoPs cannot be mandated — they must be cultivated. What are the implications for how an AI Campus Champion at a university should propose an AI literacy initiative to leadership? What language would create space for cultivation rather than imposing a mandate?

Answer: Any AI literacy initiative should be voluntary and in a “neutral space”. Those who are interested would be “invited to interact” or “invited to participate”. A focus would be on creating an open community guided by the interests of the members, rather than a leadership-imposed mandate. 

* Q4: Wenger et al. identify 'knowledge stewarding' as a key CoP function — the active curation and sharing of the community's knowledge. What knowledge management structures (shared resources, meeting rhythms, documentation practices) would an AI Campus Champion network need to function as a genuine CoP?

Answer: **Shared resources** would include a way to share information, such as a website/repository to store and disseminate information. There should also be places for members to share questions and ideas. **Meeting rhythms** should be weekly, bi-weekly, or monthly for familiarity. Anything more frequent requires too much time demand on members. Anything less frequent allows too much time to go by between interactions and reduces familiarity and “aliveness.” **Documentation** practices should include a coordinator who can be expected to devote time to the process. Documentation practices should support core, active, and peripheral participation, as well as insider and outsider perspectives.

| Community of practice | Voluntary, self-directed group of people who share a common interest and interact regularly to develop shared deliverables (resources, tools, ideas, etc.). |

| Community (CoP) | The individuals in the group and their relationships to one another |
| Practice (CoP) | The products the community develops and shares (resources, tools, ideas, etc.) |

| Legitimate peripheral participation | Peripheral participants are those who only become active when their interests come to the forefront. They may not have time to participate as much as the core or active members, but their perspectives are still important and valued.  |

### Reading 2: [A revision of Bloom's taxonomy: An overview](https://www.ou.edu/content/dam/assessment/docs/Theory%20Into%20Practice.pdf) (Krathwohl, 2002\)

What to look for as you read:

* The cognitive process dimension (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create) and the knowledge dimension (Factual, Conceptual, Procedural, Metacognitive). Both dimensions apply to learning objective design.  
* The specific cognitive process verbs associated with each level — these are the verbs used to write the learning objectives for each module of this workshop.  
* The distinction between 'Apply' (use in familiar situations) and 'Analyze' (break into constituent parts and examine relationships) — this distinction explains why Module 2 (Apply) must precede Module 4 (Analyze).  
* Metacognitive knowledge as the highest knowledge dimension: 'knowledge of one's own cognition and about oneself in relation to various subject matters.' Your Module 6 concept map revision is explicitly a metacognitive task.

* Q2: The original Bloom's taxonomy (Bloom, 1956\) used nouns (Knowledge, Comprehension, Application...); Anderson & Krathwohl revised it to use verbs (Remember, Understand, Apply...). Why does the verb formulation produce more measurable learning objectives?

The use of verbs defines actions students must perform. 

* Q3: Anderson & Krathwohl add a fourth knowledge type — Metacognitive Knowledge — to the three in Bloom's original (Factual, Conceptual, Procedural). What is metacognitive knowledge? Give one example of a learning outcome in your professional domain that targets metacognitive knowledge.

Answer: Metacognitive knowledge is knowledge about cognition (mental processes through which the brain acquires, stores, retrieves, and uses information) in general as well as awareness of and knowledge about one’s own cognition. One example of a learning outcome in physiology that targets metacognitive knowledge is, “Students will evaluate their logic when solving endocrinology case studies to identify recurring conceptual errors in their understanding of negative feedback loops, thereby developing better self-knowledge of their own cognitive gaps." 

* Q4: When designing an AI literacy learning experience for your colleagues, at which Bloom's level would you start — and why? What evidence from this workshop's design would you cite to justify your choice?

Answer: The logical place to start would be the Understand level. This is where the factual knowledge (foundational language and concepts) is explored. This workshop started with foundational knowledge and is now moving into higher dimensions of Bloom’s Taxonomy.
